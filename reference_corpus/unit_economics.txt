Unit economics is the analysis of profit or loss for a single unit of business activity, most commonly one order, one customer or one transaction. The approach breaks revenue and cost down to this smallest repeatable level so that a company can see whether growth itself creates value or merely multiplies losses.For an online grocery order, the calculation typically starts with the revenue from the basket, adds any delivery or handling fees, then subtracts the cost of goods sold, picking and packing labour, payment processing charges, rider payment and an allocated share of rent and utilities. What remains is the contribution margin per order. A contribution margin that stays negative means every additional order increases the total loss, regardless of how fast the order book grows.Customer-level unit economics extend the same logic across the lifetime of a customer. They compare the customer acquisition cost with the expected number of orders over the retention period and the margin from each. A business can accept negative margins on early orders if repeat behaviour later turns the relationship profitable, but the assumption of long retention must be tested against observed churn rather than projected optimistically.Analysts reading unit economics pay attention to which costs are excluded. If rent, technology development or central salaries are left out of the per-order figure, the reported contribution overstates true profitability. A complete picture therefore reports both the contribution margin of a marginal order and the fully loaded result once all fixed costs are distributed across actual volume.
