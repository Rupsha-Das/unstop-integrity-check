Customer acquisition cost, usually abbreviated CAC, is the average amount a business spends to gain one new customer. It is calculated by dividing all sales and marketing expenses in a period by the number of new customers acquired in that period. The expenses included typically cover advertising, promotional discounts, referral incentives, sales salaries and agency fees.Expenses are expenses; the definition matters mainly in what is counted as expense. Some companies report a blended CAC that spreads marketing spend across all customers gained, including organic ones, while others report a paid CAC that only counts customers reached through paid campaigns. Blended figures are smaller and look better, so comparisons between companies should check which definition is used.The standard rule of thumb compares CAC with the lifetime value of a customer, LTV. A commonly cited healthy ratio is three to one, meaning a customer's expected contribution over their lifetime is triple what it cost to acquire them. The payback period, the time needed for cumulative margin to repay the acquisition cost, is often considered more informative than the ratio itself, because a business with rapid payback can reinvest quickly even at a modest ratio.For subscription and delivery businesses, CAC tends to rise as a market saturates, since the easiest customers are acquired first and later growth must reach less interested or harder-to-serve users. When discount-heavy acquisition is used, analysts watch second-order behaviour: whether customers obtained through large first-order discounts continue ordering after promotions end, or churn immediately.
